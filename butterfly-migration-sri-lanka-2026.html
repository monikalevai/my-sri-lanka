When butterflies fill the sky: Sri Lanka’s remarkable seasonal migration

Every year, thousands of butterflies take to the skies above Sri Lanka. But this November, their extraordinary journey is attracting attention across the island.

Imagine looking up on an ordinary morning and seeing hundreds of delicate yellow and white butterflies drifting through the air, all travelling in the

same direction.

For people across Sri Lanka, this beautiful sight has become a reality.

In November 2026, large numbers of butterflies have been spotted over Colombo and many other parts of the island. Videos of their movements have spread across social media, leaving many people wondering where these tiny travellers are going and why they have appeared in such remarkable numbers.

The answer lies in one of Sri Lanka’s fascinating seasonal natural phenomena.

A journey guided by
the monsoon

Sri Lanka experiences two main periods of seasonal butterfly migration.

The larger movement generally takes place between October and December, during the second inter-monsoon period. A smaller migration occurs around March and April.

These journeys are closely connected to the island’s changing weather.

As monsoon rains arrive, plants begin producing fresh leaves, creating ideal conditions for caterpillars to feed and butterflies to reproduce.

When butterfly populations increase, many begin travelling in search of new feeding and
breeding areas.

The result can be extraordinary: large groups of butterflies moving across the landscape, sometimes appearing like a stream of tiny wings flowing through

the sky.

The yellow and
white travellers

Several species are associated with these seasonal movements, including:

  • Lemon Emigrant (Catopsilia pomona), a yellowish butterfly commonly seen across Sri Lanka.
  • Lesser Albatross (Appias galene), a predominantly white butterfly.
  • Common Albatross (Appias albina), a species also associated with seasonal movements.

Although each butterfly appears delicate, their collective journeys can cover remarkable distances.

Research published in 2024 documented Albatross butterfly movements along a route of approximately 280 kilometres, from the southeastern part of Sri Lanka towards the northwest.

Researchers estimated that the journey could take just three to four days.

For creatures with such fragile-looking wings, it is an impressive achievement.

The legend of
Butterfly Mountain

Long before scientists began studying butterfly migration, the people of Sri Lanka had their own explanation for these mysterious journeys.

According to a traditional belief, butterflies travel towards Sri Pada, also known as Adam’s Peak.

The mountain is sometimes called Samanala Kanda, or Butterfly Mountain.

For generations, people have associated the seasonal appearance of butterflies with their journey towards this
sacred mountain.

It is a beautiful image: thousands of tiny creatures making their way towards one of Sri Lanka’s most spiritually
significant peaks.

Science offers another perspective.

Some butterflies exhibit a behaviour known as hill-topping, gathering around elevated areas where they may have better opportunities to find mates.

However, research indicates that many migrating butterflies travel northwest rather than directly towards Sri Pada.

The traditional story and scientific explanation offer two different ways of understanding a phenomenon that has fascinated people for generations.

Are the butterflies warning of a natural disaster?

The unusually noticeable migration in November 2026 has also sparked speculation online.

Some people have wondered whether large numbers of butterflies could signal an approaching cyclone or another
natural disaster.

Scientists have found no reliable evidence that butterfly migrations predict

such events.

Butterflies are sensitive to environmental conditions, including temperature, humidity, wind and atmospheric pressure. But their seasonal movements are primarily associated with rainfall, plant growth and reproduction.

Rather than a warning of disaster, the current migration is part of a recurring natural cycle.

A beautiful phenomenon facing an uncertain future

Despite the impressive sightings this November, researchers have raised concerns about the long-term decline of butterfly populations and large
migration events.

Changing rainfall patterns can disrupt breeding cycles, while habitat loss and invasive plants threaten the vegetation that caterpillars depend on.

Without suitable host plants, future generations of butterflies may struggle

to survive.

Protecting native vegetation and natural habitats is therefore essential to preserving these seasonal journeys.

A reminder of Sri Lanka’s natural wonders

Sri Lanka is celebrated for its elephants, leopards, tropical forests and

extraordinary birdlife.

Yet sometimes, one of its most memorable wildlife experiences comes from creatures small enough to rest on a single leaf.

The seasonal butterfly migration is a reminder that the island’s natural wonders are not always found deep inside
national parks.

Sometimes, they are passing quietly above a busy street, drifting across a garden or filling the sky on an otherwise
ordinary afternoon.

And for a few fleeting
moments, the everyday world becomes
something extraordinary.
