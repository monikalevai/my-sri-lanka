Anuradhapura: A city of sacred ruins

Anuradhapura is a place where Sri Lanka’s ancient past still feels remarkably close. Across its vast landscape, sacred sites welcome pilgrims and the remains of a once-powerful kingdom tell a story that began more than two thousand years ago.

This is not a city you simply look at. It is a place you move through slowly, discovering history, faith and everyday life woven together across one extraordinary landscape.

A city built around faith

Some places tell their
history through museums.

Anuradhapura tells it through enormous stupas rising above the trees, ancient stone structures, quiet pathways and sacred places where people still come to pray.

For more than a thousand years, Anuradhapura was one of the most important political and religious centres in Sri Lanka. It flourished for around 1,300 years before being abandoned after an invasion in 993, and for centuries much of the ancient city remained hidden beneath the jungle. Today, its monasteries, monuments and sacred sites form one of Sri Lanka’s most important UNESCO World Heritage sites.

But Anuradhapura does not feel like a city that belongs only to the past.

It is still a place of prayer, pilgrimage and everyday devotion.

The sacred Sri Maha Bodhi

At the heart of Anuradhapura’s spiritual identity stands the Sri Maha Bodhi.

According to Buddhist tradition, it grew from a cutting of the Bodhi tree associated with the Buddha’s enlightenment, brought to Sri Lanka in the 3rd century BC by Sanghamitta. For more than two thousand years, the tree has remained one of the country’s most important Buddhist sites and continues to draw pilgrims from across Sri
Lanka and beyond.

There is something remarkable about a place where history is not kept behind glass.

Here, the past is still part of the present.

People come with flowers and offerings. They pray, sit quietly and take part in traditions that have continued for centuries.

That is what makes Anuradhapura feel different from simply walking through a collection of ancient ruins.

The great dagobas

The skyline of ancient Anuradhapura is shaped by its enormous dagobas, also known as stupas.

Ruwanwelisaya, Jetavanaramaya and Abhayagiriya are among the most impressive structures in the ancient city. Their scale alone gives an idea of how important Anuradhapura once was.

These were not small temples hidden
in the jungle.

They were part of vast monastic complexes and a city built around religion, learning and community. The remains of monasteries, stone structures, ponds and carvings still spread across a huge area, making Anuradhapura feel less like one attraction and more like an entire lost world waiting to be explored.

And yet, despite their age, these places are not silent monuments.

Many of them remain deeply sacred.

More than ruins

One of the things I find most fascinating about Anuradhapura is that it was once a highly sophisticated city.

Beyond the famous stupas were monasteries, gardens, ponds and an advanced system of reservoirs and irrigation. Water played a central role in the development of the ancient city, particularly because this part of Sri Lanka lies in
the dry zone.

The engineering behind these systems was remarkably advanced, and parts of the ancient water infrastructure still survive today. Places such as the Twin Ponds, or Kuttam Pokuna, offer another glimpse into the knowledge and planning that shaped life here centuries ago.

It is easy to look at the enormous monuments first.

But the details matter too.

A carved stone.

A quiet pond.

A line of ancient pillars.

They are reminders that Anuradhapura was once a living city filled with people, routines, rituals and everyday life.

A place to slow down

Anuradhapura is not really a place
to rush through.

There is simply too much to see, and much of its atmosphere lies in the spaces between the famous landmarks.

The roads stretch between sacred sites. Monkeys move through the trees. Pilgrims dressed in white gather around temples. Huge stupas appear above the landscape, sometimes long before you reach them.

This is one of those destinations where slowing down is part of the experience.

You could spend hours moving from one important monument to another and still feel as though you have only seen part of the ancient city.

And perhaps that is the point.

Anuradhapura is not asking you
to see everything.

It is asking you to take your time.

Where the past is still alive

Calling Anuradhapura a city of
ruins is accurate.

But it is not the whole story.

Yes, there are ancient structures that have stood for centuries. There are stone foundations, broken pillars and the remains of a once powerful kingdom.

But there is also a living religious landscape.

Thousands of people continue to visit Anuradhapura as pilgrims, and Buddhist traditions remain part of the city’s present. That connection between ancient history and modern devotion is one of the reasons the place feels so powerful.

The past has not disappeared here.

It is still being remembered.

Still being protected.

Still being lived.

Why Anuradhapura
stays with you

Some destinations impress you with one unforgettable view.

Anuradhapura works differently.

Its beauty comes from the scale of
the whole place.

A sacred tree that has been honoured for centuries. Enormous white and brick stupas rising above the landscape. Ancient monasteries. Quiet ponds.
Pilgrims carrying flowers.

And everywhere, the feeling that you are walking through a place where history
never completely ended.

Anuradhapura is not just a city you visit to learn about Sri Lanka’s past. It is a place where the past is still part of the present.
